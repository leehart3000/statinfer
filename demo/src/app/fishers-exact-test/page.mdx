## When to use Fisher's exact test

Fisher's exact test answers the same question as a [chi-square test](/chi-square-test) of independence on a 2×2 table: are the rows and columns related? For example, does a treatment (rows: treated or not) affect recovery (columns: recovered or not)?

The difference is that it doesn't rely on an approximation. It works out the exact probability of every possible table with the same row and column totals. That makes it reliable **even when the counts are small**, where the chi-square test and the [proportion test](/proportion-test) can be misleading.

The demo starts with the experiment Fisher invented the test for. A colleague claimed she could taste whether milk had been poured into a cup before or after the tea. She was given 8 cups, 4 of each, and correctly identified 3 of the 4 "milk first" cups. The rows are the true order, and the columns are her guesses. Could she really tell, or was she guessing? Notice that with only 8 cups, even a very good performance isn't strong evidence.

The **odds ratio** measures the strength of the relationship: 1 means no relationship, and larger values mean the diagonal counts (top left and bottom right) are bigger than you'd expect.

Fisher's exact test has no statistic like t or chi-square. It's based directly on the top-left count, so that's what the table's **statistic** shows. The **estimate** is the odds ratio in your table: (top left × bottom right) ÷ (top right × bottom left).

### The raw result

`fishersExactTest` returns a plain JavaScript object, which your code can use directly. For example, `result.estimate` is the odds ratio. Below, the same object is shown as **JSON** (JavaScript Object Notation), a standard text format for data, which you can save or send to other programs. The odds ratio is `Infinity` when only the bottom half of its fraction is zero, and `null` when it's undefined. There's no confidence interval yet: an exact interval for the odds ratio is planned for a future version.
